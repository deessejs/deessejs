# How we ship a template: the editorial workflow behind the registry

Every template in the registry goes through the same five-stage review before it's marked GA. The process is slow on purpose. Templates are forever — once we ship one, we maintain it indefinitely, and the cost of shipping a bad template is paid by every customer who adopts it.

## Stage 1: RFC

Every new template starts as an RFC. The RFC is a one-page document that covers:

- The surface area (SaaS, mobile, CLIs, etc.).
- The contracts it would implement.
- The providers it would default to.
- The edge cases it would handle.
- The rough size of the work, in weeks.

The RFC is reviewed by two people: one engineer who has shipped a similar template before, and one operator who has run a similar template in production. The review happens in a comment thread, not a meeting.

The RFC is approved when both reviewers agree that:

- The surface area is something the registry should cover.
- The contracts are stable enough that the template can implement them.
- The work fits in a quarter.

If any of those three is false, the RFC is rejected or sent back for revision. We have rejected roughly half of the RFCs we've received.

## Stage 2: scaffold

Once the RFC is approved, the work moves to scaffolding. The scaffolding engineer writes the template's basic structure:

- The directory tree.
- The `deessejs.json` manifest.
- The AGENTS.md file.
- The README.

The scaffolding is reviewed by the same two people who reviewed the RFC. The review focuses on: does the structure match what the RFC promised? Is the AGENTS.md small enough to fit in a context window? Does the manifest accurately describe the contracts?

The scaffolding is approved when both reviewers agree that the structure is what the RFC described. We have never rejected a scaffold that was approved at the RFC stage, because the RFC's structure review catches issues before the work starts.

## Stage 3: contracts

Once the scaffolding is approved, the engineer implements each contract. The contracts are reviewed contract by contract, not as a single PR.

For each contract:

- The implementation is reviewed by the engineer who maintains that contract in the registry.
- The reviewer checks: does the implementation match the contract's interface? Are the edge cases handled? Are the tests covering the failure paths?
- The reviewer runs the template locally and exercises the contract end to end.

Each contract is approved independently. A template can ship with three of four contracts implemented if the fourth is blocking on something external.

## Stage 4: dogfood

Once all the contracts are approved, the engineer dogfoods the template. They build a small product on top of it — usually a CLI for an internal tool, or a dashboard for an internal service.

The dogfood catches the things that reviews miss:

- The setup steps that don't quite work on a fresh machine.
- The commands that fail silently.
- The defaults that are wrong for a real product.

The dogfood is a minimum of two weeks. Longer if the template has more surface area.

The engineer writes a dogfood report: what worked, what didn't, what they changed. The report is reviewed by the same two people who reviewed the RFC. The review focuses on: is the template now ready for a customer to adopt?

## Stage 5: GA

Once the dogfood report is approved, the template is marked GA. The marking happens in the registry's manifest, with a version number and a date.

The GA announcement includes:

- The release notes.
- The migration guide for anyone using the previous version of the template.
- The list of breaking changes (if any).
- The link to the docs.

After GA, the template enters maintenance mode. New contracts can be added in minor versions. Breaking changes require a major version and a migration guide.

## What we don't do

We don't ship templates without dogfood. Every template in the registry has been used internally for at least two weeks.

We don't ship templates without an AGENTS.md. If the AGENTS.md isn't there, the template doesn't ship.

We don't ship templates without tests for the failure paths. A test that exercises the happy path is not enough.

We don't ship templates that require manual setup steps. If the README says "run this command, then edit this file, then do this other thing," the template isn't ready.

## What we got wrong the first time

Our first template went through a one-stage review: "is it done?" It was not done. It shipped with manual setup steps. We spent a quarter cleaning it up.

The five-stage process is the response. It is slow on purpose. It catches the things that the first template missed.

## How long does this take

Roughly:

- RFC: 1-2 weeks.
- Scaffold: 1-2 weeks.
- Contracts: 4-8 weeks (varies by surface area).
- Dogfood: 2-4 weeks.
- GA: 1 week (announcement, migration guide).

Total: 2-4 months from RFC to GA, depending on the template's complexity.

A template that takes less time is suspicious. A template that takes more time is normal.

## The boring conclusion

The registry is small on purpose. We have shipped five templates in two years. We are not trying to ship a hundred.

Each template is a multi-month investment. Each template is maintained forever. Each template is reviewed five times before it ships.

The process is slow. The process is right. The cost of shipping a bad template is paid by every customer who adopts it, and we don't want to pay that cost.
